One caveat, stated up front

No assessment of this child has happened yet, and the specimen paper has not been released. Sections 1 to 4, 8 to 17 and the appendices hold whatever the diagnostic shows. Section 7, the topic order, is a default sequence that gets reordered at the end of Session 1 and again when the specimen paper arrives. That reordering is expected, not a failure of the plan.

Section 1

The exam, and what it is really testing

Read the specification closely and the content list is unremarkable. Everything named sits inside the Year 5 and Year 6 curriculum. The difficulty is not in the topics.

The exam is 50 minutes, split into a multiple choice section worth about 40 per cent of the marks and a written section worth about 60 per cent. No calculator. The content spans four strands: Number, Algebra, Shape, Handling Data.

Three features of that design decide how the preparation should work.

It is an arithmetic test wearing four different hats

Remove the calculator and almost every question, whatever strand it sits in, ends in a piece of mental or written arithmetic. Area of a triangle is one multiplication and one halving. Percentage of an amount is a division and a multiplication. Mean is an addition and a division. A child who is secure on method but slow or inaccurate with number will lose marks across all four strands at once, and the mark scheme will make those look like four separate weaknesses when they are one.

This is why arithmetic fluency, not topic coverage, is the spine of the plan.

Fifty minutes is short

Fifty minutes across two sections means the child cannot afford to stall. Most candidates at this level lose more marks to unfinished papers and careless slips than to topics they have never met. Pace is a trainable skill and it is trained by repeated timed exposure, not by being told to work faster.

Multiple choice is a different exam from written answers

Section A rewards speed, estimation, elimination and working backwards from the options. Section B rewards laid-out method, because method marks exist there and nowhere else. These need separating and practising as two distinct skills. A child drilled only on written method will be slow in Section A. A child drilled only on multiple choice will write bare answers in Section B and lose the method marks.

What the plan therefore prioritises, in order

  1. Arithmetic accuracy and speed without a calculator
  2. Fractions, decimals, percentages and ratio, which carry the heaviest mark load in Number
  3. Timed exam exposure, starting early and repeating often
  4. Area, perimeter, angles and averages, which are high yield and quick to secure
  5. Algebra and chart interpretation, which are narrow at this level and can be taught late
Section 2

Five decisions the plan is built on

1. The hour teaches. The week practises.

Fifteen hours of tuition between now and January is not enough on its own. Twenty minutes a day, five days a week, over sixteen weeks is roughly twenty-six hours of additional practice. The home practice is where fluency is built. The lesson is where method is taught, errors are diagnosed and technique is rehearsed. If the lesson is spent on drill that could have happened at home, the hour has been wasted.

2. Diagnose before teaching anything.

Session 1 is a full baseline, not an introduction. Without it the topic order is guesswork, and with one hour a week there is no room for guesswork. The diagnostic is in Section 5 and is designed to produce a per-strand score, not a single mark, so the output is a priority list rather than a grade.

3. Space the retrieval.

A topic taught in week three and never revisited is gone by January. Every home practice week includes two retrieval days pulling from earlier topics, and every lesson opens with a timed starter drawn from previous weeks. Nothing is taught once.

4. Practise under exam conditions from early on, not just at the end.

There are two 25-minute half-papers in the first half of term and three full mocks after it. The first timed experience should not be in January. Timing pressure changes what a child can do, and the gap between untimed and timed performance is itself a thing to be measured and closed.

5. Work from errors, not from topics.

After Session 1, what gets taught is decided by what the child actually gets wrong, logged in the error book and re-tested on a schedule. The scheme of work in Section 7 is the default. The error book overrides it.

What this plan deliberately does not do

  • It does not cover Year 7 or Year 8 content. The specification is explicit that the paper sits at National Curriculum levels 4 and 5. Teaching beyond that buys nothing and costs confidence.
  • It does not increase the volume of home practice as January approaches. Twenty minutes a day is the whole term. Volume spikes in the final fortnight produce fatigue, not marks.
  • It does not use a calculator at any point, including for checking. The exam does not, so the preparation does not.

Section 4

The standard hour

Same shape every week, so no time is lost to settling in and the child knows what is coming. Sessions 1, 13, 14 and 15 are the exceptions and have their own run sheets.

MinutesBlockWhat happens
0 to 6Timed starterThe week's arithmetic sprint, 40 items, against the clock. Marked immediately and the score logged. Then three spaced retrieval questions from earlier topics, taken from the error book.
6 to 13Error reviewOnly the questions the child got wrong in the week's home practice. Correct answers are not revisited. For each error: what was done, what should have been done, one sentence naming the rule. Logged in the error book.
13 to 36Teaching blockOne topic. Worked example on the page while the child narrates it back, then a guided pair, then four to six independent questions with me watching the working rather than the answers.
36 to 50Exam-format practiceThe same topic in exam form and against a clock: six multiple choice at 45 seconds each, then two written questions at three minutes each. This block is where the topic becomes exam-ready.
50 to 57RepairWhatever the exam-format block exposed, fixed on the spot. If nothing was exposed, an extension question one level harder.
57 to 60Set and closeHome practice handed over, the week's two retrieval topics named, and one thing the child has to be able to explain back to a parent by Friday.

Three rules for the hour

  • The child holds the pen in the teaching block. If I am writing and they are watching, they are not learning the method, they are watching me remember it.
  • Every wrong answer gets explained before it gets corrected. The wrong answer usually contains the misconception, and the misconception is the thing worth treating.
  • Nothing in the hour is untimed except the teaching block. Working against a clock has to become ordinary rather than an event.

Online or in person

If sessions run online, two adjustments. First, the child needs a second device or a phone camera pointed at their page, so I can see working as it happens rather than after it. Second, timed blocks are run with a visible shared timer, not a verbal countdown. Everything else holds.

Part 3: Section B, six written questions, 25 minutes

30 marks. Method must be shown. Marks are awarded for method even where the final answer is wrong.

B1. Written methods (4 marks)

Work these out. Show your method.

(a) 3,684 + 2,957    (b) 5,002 − 1,438    (c) 236 × 7    (d) 918 ÷ 6

B2. Percentage of an amount (4 marks)

B3. Proportion (3 marks)

B5. Sequences and formulae (6 marks)

How to read the result

Sprint, out of 40 in four minutes. 36 or more: fluency is secure, the drill stays as maintenance. 28 to 35: daily drill is necessary but the scheme of work runs as written. Below 28: fluency is the binding constraint. Cut two teaching topics from the scheme and give the recovered time to arithmetic until the score reaches 32.

Any strand. Below 50 per cent: teach from the beginning, allow two sessions. 50 to 79 per cent: one session to consolidate, then retrieval only. 80 per cent or above: no teaching session, retrieval questions in starters only, and use the freed session on a weaker strand.

In a multiple choice section the distractors are not random. Each one corresponds to a specific error, so a wrong answer names the misconception. This table is what makes the diagnostic worth more than a score.

Section 7

Scheme of work, session by session

Sessions 2 to 12 run on the standard hour from Section 4. What changes is the teaching block and the exam-format block. Sessions 13, 14 and 15 have their own run sheets. The order below is the default and is rewritten at the end of Session 1 against the strand grid.

Session 2w/c 28 Sep · Number
Written methods for all four operations, rounding, and order of operations.

Teach: column addition and subtraction including decimals of unequal length and exchanging across zeros; short multiplication; two-digit by two-digit multiplication; short division including remainders expressed as decimals; rounding to 10, 100, 1,000 and one decimal place; brackets, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.

Errors to pre-empt: decimal points not aligned; 5,002 minus 1,438 style exchanging; remainders left as r 3 when a decimal is wanted; working left to right through a mixed operation string.

Home practice. Sprint sheet A daily. Mon and Tue: 12 column calculations. Wed: retrieval, the six diagnostic errors. Thu: 10 rounding, plus order of operations. Fri: 6 multiple choice and 2 written, timed. Explain back by Friday: why you line up the decimal points.
Session 3w/c 5 Oct · Number
Place value, decimals, and negative numbers.

Teach: multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000 as digits moving rather than the point moving; ordering decimals of unequal length; adding and subtracting negatives on a number line; temperature and difference problems; multiplying by a negative.

Errors to pre-empt: 0.7 judged smaller than 0.35 because it has fewer digits; minus 6 plus 9 treated as minus 15; the difference between minus 3 and 5 given as 2.

Home practice. Sprint sheet B daily. Mon and Tue: place value and decimals. Wed: retrieval, Session 2 written methods. Thu: negatives, plus error-book re-test. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: how to tell which of two decimals is larger.
Session 4w/c 12 Oct · Number
Factors, multiples, primes, square numbers.

Teach: listing factors in pairs so none are missed; multiples and lowest common multiple; highest common factor, which is the tool fractions will need next week; primes up to 50 and why 1 is not one; square numbers to 144 and their roots; divisibility tests for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9 and 10.

Errors to pre-empt: factors and multiples used interchangeably; 1 and the number itself omitted from a factor list; 51 and 91 assumed prime.

Home practice. Sprint sheet C daily. Mon and Tue: factors, multiples, primes. Wed: retrieval, Session 2. Thu: squares and divisibility. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: the difference between a factor and a multiple, in one sentence each.
Section 7, continued

Sessions 5 to 7

Session 5w/c 19 Oct · Number
Fractions: equivalence, simplifying, of an amount, adding and subtracting.

Teach: equivalent fractions; simplifying fully using the highest common factor from last week; improper fractions and mixed numbers both ways; fraction of an amount as divide by the bottom, multiply by the top; adding and subtracting with a common denominator; ordering fractions by converting to a common denominator.

Errors to pre-empt: stopping after one simplification step; adding denominators; dividing and forgetting to multiply.

Home practice. Sprint sheet D daily. Mon and Tue: simplifying and fraction of an amount. Wed: retrieval, Session 3 negatives. Thu: adding and subtracting fractions. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: how to find three eighths of 64 without a calculator.
Session 6w/c 26 Oct · Number · half term
Percentages, and moving between fractions, decimals and percentages.

Teach: the non-calculator toolkit, 50% by halving, 25% by quartering, 10% by dividing by ten, 5% as half of 10%, 1% by dividing by a hundred, and building everything else by combining those; percentage of an amount; percentage increase and decrease including sale prices; the eight conversions to know cold, being a half, a quarter, three quarters, a fifth, two fifths, a tenth, a third and a hundredth.

Errors to pre-empt: finding the reduction and giving it as the sale price; 15% found by halving 30%; a third written as 0.3.

Home practice. Sprint sheet E daily. Mon and Tue: percentage of an amount. Wed: retrieval, Session 4. Thu: increase, decrease and conversions. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: three different ways to find 15% of a number without a calculator.
Session 7w/c 2 Nov · Number · half-paper 1
Ratio and proportion, then the first timed assessment.

Teach (25 min): ratio notation; total parts, one part, then scale; sharing an amount in a ratio; scaling a recipe up and down; the unitary method; simple best value.

Half-paper 1 (25 min): 10 multiple choice and 4 written, Number only, everything from Sessions 2 to 7. Marked in the last 10 minutes with the child. Record the score and, separately, how many questions were unattempted, because those are two different problems.

Errors to pre-empt: treating 2:3 as two thirds; giving the wrong person's share; scaling by adding rather than multiplying.

Home practice. Sprint, best sheet so far. Mon and Tue: ratio and proportion. Wed and Thu: repair from half-paper 1, nothing else. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: how you know which share belongs to whom.
Section 7, continued

Sessions 8 to 10

Session 8w/c 9 Nov · Shape
Perimeter and area of rectangles, triangles, parallelograms and compound shapes.

Teach: perimeter as distance round and area as space inside, established once and returned to every time; rectangle; triangle as half base times perpendicular height, including when the height is drawn outside the triangle; parallelogram as base times perpendicular height, and why it is not the slanted side; compound shapes by splitting and by subtracting; finding a missing side when the area is given; the fact that an L-shape has the same perimeter as its bounding rectangle.

Errors to pre-empt: area calculated when perimeter is asked for; the halving forgotten in a triangle; the slant side used as the height of a parallelogram; units given as cm rather than cm squared.

Home practice. Sprint daily. Mon and Tue: area and perimeter. Wed: retrieval, Session 5 fractions. Thu: compound shapes. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: why the height of a parallelogram is not the sloping side.
Session 9w/c 16 Nov · Shape
Angles, symmetry, properties of shapes, metric units.

Teach: angles on a straight line summing to 180, at a point to 360, in a triangle to 180; isosceles and equilateral triangles; naming and describing quadrilaterals; lines of symmetry and order of rotational symmetry; metric conversion between mm, cm, m and km, between g and kg, and between ml and l; reading a scale where the divisions are not single units.

Errors to pre-empt: the two base angles of an isosceles triangle not recognised as equal; 2.5 kg converted to 250 g; a scale read as though every division is one unit.

Home practice. Sprint daily. Mon and Tue: angles. Wed: retrieval, Session 6 percentages. Thu: symmetry and metric units. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: the three angle facts, from memory, without prompting.
Session 10w/c 23 Nov · Data · half-paper 2
Mean, median, mode and range, then the second timed assessment.

Teach (25 min): mean as total divided by how many; median with the list ordered first, and with an even number of values; mode including no mode and two modes; range as largest minus smallest; and working backwards, where the mean is given and a missing value has to be found.

Half-paper 2 (25 min): 10 multiple choice and 4 written, Shape and Data, from Sessions 8 to 10. Marked with the child.

Errors to pre-empt: median taken from the unordered list; mean and median swapped; range given as the largest value.

Home practice. Sprint daily. Mon and Tue: averages. Wed and Thu: repair from half-paper 2. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: the first thing you do before finding a median.

Sessions 11 and 12

Session 11w/c 30 Nov · Data
Bar charts, pie charts and real-life graphs.

Teach: reading a bar chart whose axis goes up in twos, fives or tens; dual bar charts and comparison questions; pie charts read by fraction of the circle and by degrees using 360 divided by the total; conversion graphs read in both directions; distance-time graphs, including what a flat section means; reading between gridlines.

Errors to pre-empt: every gridline assumed to be one unit; a pie chart sector counted as a number of pupils rather than a proportion; a graph read in one direction only.

Home practice. Sprint daily. Mon and Tue: charts. Wed: retrieval, Session 8 area. Thu: real-life graphs. Fri: timed exam set. Explain back: how to work out how many people one sector of a pie chart represents.
Session 12w/c 7 Dec · Algebra
Coordinates, sequences, formulae and inverse operations.

Teach: coordinates in all four quadrants, read and plotted, including completing a rectangle from three given points; sequences by term-to-term rule, continuing them, finding a later term, and deciding whether a given number appears; a simple position-to-term rule; substituting into a formula with two operations, with order of operations respected; inverse operations and function machines, used to work backwards from an answer.

Errors to pre-empt: x and y transposed; a negative coordinate read with the wrong sign; a two-operation formula evaluated left to right; an inverse chain undone in the wrong order.

Home practice. Sprint daily. Mon and Tue: coordinates and sequences. Wed: retrieval, Session 9 angles. Thu: formulae and inverses. Fri: full Section A practice, 20 multiple choice in 17 minutes. Explain back: how to check a coordinate is the right way round.

If the diagnostic reorders this

Section 8

Home practice: the daily twenty minutes

Five days a week, Monday to Friday, weekends free except mock weekends. Twenty minutes, at the same time each day. The consistency matters more than the content.

DayFirst 4 minutesRemaining 15 minutes
MondaySprint, timed, scored12 questions on the topic taught this week. Straightforward versions.

WednesdaySprint, timed, scoredRetrieval. 10 questions from two weeks ago.
ThursdaySprint, timed, scoredRetrieval. 10 questions from five weeks ago, plus re-test of any error-book item due.
FridaySprint, timed, scoredExam format against the clock. 6 multiple choice in 5 minutes, then 2 written questions in 8 minutes.

Why it is built this way

  • The sprint is every single day. Four minutes, sixteen weeks, is about five hours of pure fluency work. Nothing else in the plan buys as many marks per minute on a non-calculator paper.
  • Two retrieval days a week. Two weeks back and five weeks back. Without these, September and October are gone by Christmas.
  • Friday is always timed and always in exam format. Sixteen Fridays means sixteen rehearsals before January. Exam conditions should be boring by then.
  • Twenty minutes is a ceiling, not a target to beat. If a set is finished in twelve minutes, the day is done. Adding more is how children come to resent the subject in November, when there is still a term to go.

Marking

A parent marks it the same day, ticks and crosses only, no explanation offered and no re-teaching attempted. A cross gets circled and goes in the error book untouched. The reason for the no-re-teaching rule is not that parents cannot teach it. It is that a method explained differently from the one taught in the lesson produces a child holding two half-methods, and that is worse than holding one.

If the child is stuck and distressed, the instruction is to stop, write the question number on a sticky note, and move on. I would rather see the stuck question than a tearful forty minutes.

The weekend

Free, except the two mock Saturdays, 9 January and any additional one agreed. Protecting the weekend is what makes five days a week sustainable for sixteen weeks.

Section 9

The error book

One exercise book, used for nothing else. It is the single most important object in this plan, because it is what turns fifteen hours of tuition into a targeted fifteen hours rather than a generic fifteen.

What goes in it

Every question the child gets wrong, in a lesson, in home practice, in a half-paper or in a mock. Nothing that was got right. One entry per error, four lines:

Line 1. The question, copied or stuck in.

Line 2. What I did.

Line 3. What I should have done.

Line 4. The rule, in one sentence, in the child's own words.

Line 2 is the one people skip and the one that matters. The wrong working is where the misconception lives. Line 4 has to be the child's sentence, not mine, because a rule they cannot phrase is a rule they have not got.

Re-testing

Each entry is dated and re-tested twice: seven days later and twenty-one days later, on the Thursday retrieval slot. A re-test that is correct both times gets the entry ruled through. A re-test that is wrong resets the clock and, if it happens twice, the topic goes into the next lesson's teaching block regardless of what the scheme of work says.

The category count

Every four weeks, count the entries by category rather than reading them. The categories that matter for this paper are: recall speed, written method slip, misread the question, method not known, method known but misapplied, units or labelling, and ran out of time. The distribution tells you what to fix, and it changes what to fix in a way that reading individual questions does not.

Section 10

Section A technique: multiple choice

Roughly 40 per cent of the marks. It is a different skill from the written section and gets trained separately, from Session 2 onward, not saved for January.

The seven rules

  1. Cover the options. Solve. Then look. Distractors are designed to match the specific errors a child is likely to make, so reading them first pulls you toward one. Working the answer out blind and then finding it in the list removes that pull entirely. This is the single most valuable habit in Section A.
  2. If your answer is not there, you have made an error, not found a printing mistake. Go back and check the last operation first.
  3. Estimate before calculating on anything with decimals or large numbers. An estimate usually eliminates two options in five seconds and catches place value errors that would otherwise be invisible.
  4. For find-the-value questions, substitute the options back in. Four substitutions are often faster and safer than one rearrangement.
  5. Two passes. First pass, answer everything that takes under 45 seconds and put a star beside anything that does not. Second pass, return to the starred ones with the time that is left. Never let one question eat four minutes on the first pass.
  6. Nothing is left blank. On the second pass, eliminate what is impossible and choose from the rest. A one in three guess is worth more than an empty box, and there is no penalty for a wrong answer on papers of this type.
  7. Check the units and check it is sensible. A perimeter answer in cm squared is wrong. A sale price higher than the original price is wrong. Ten seconds of sense-checking recovers more marks than ten seconds of extra calculation.

How this gets trained

Every lesson's exam-format block includes six multiple choice questions at 45 seconds each, with a visible timer. Every Friday's home practice does the same. Session 14 runs an intensive of 30 questions in 20 minutes. By January the child will have done somewhere near 400 timed multiple choice questions, and 45 seconds will feel like a normal amount of time rather than a threat.

Section 11

Section B technique: written answers

Roughly 60 per cent of the marks, and the only place method marks exist. A child who writes bare answers here is throwing away marks they have already earned.

The six rules

  1. Write the calculation, not just the answer. Even where the answer is obvious. On a four-mark question, two of those marks are usually for method, and a wrong final answer with correct working still scores. A right answer with no working can score full marks, but a wrong one with no working scores zero.
  2. Read the last line of the question first. It tells you what is being asked, which tells you what to look for in the rest of it.
  3. Underline the command word and the units. Find, calculate, explain, and how many all want different things. Explain means a sentence, and a number alone will not score.
  4. Units on every answer, every time. Pounds, centimetres, centimetres squared, grams, degrees. This is free marks and it is lost constantly.
  5. Lay working out downward, not across the page. One step per line. It is easier to check, easier to mark, and it is where the method marks are found.
  6. Answer the question that was asked. If it asks for the sale price, do not stop at the discount. If it asks for Ben's share, do not give Ana's.

Explain questions

Questions asking why or how you know appear on papers of this kind and are routinely half-answered. The structure to drill is: state the answer, then give the mathematical reason, in one sentence. For B5(a)(iii) in the diagnostic, no on its own is half a mark's worth of thinking. No, because the terms go 43, 48, 53 and 51 is not among them, is the full answer. This gets practised from Session 2 through the explain-back task in every week's home practice.

The two-minute check at the end

If two minutes remain at the end of the paper, they are spent in this order, and not on re-doing questions. First, every blank filled in Section A. Second, units checked on every Section B answer. Third, any answer that is obviously unreasonable, a negative length, a percentage over a hundred, a sale price above the original, reconsidered. Re-working a question you already answered is almost always a worse use of two minutes than these three checks.

Section 12

Owning the fifty minutes

Pacing is a skill and it is trainable. Most marks lost at this level are lost to unfinished papers and rushed slips rather than to unknown topics.

The timing plan

The exact number of questions is not known until the specimen paper arrives, so this is provisional and gets confirmed then. Working from the published weighting of 40 per cent multiple choice and 60 per cent written:

BlockTimeRule
Section A, pass 114 minutesEverything that takes under 45 seconds. Star and skip the rest. Do not stop to finish a hard one.
Section A, pass 24 minutesThe starred questions. Eliminate and choose. Nothing left blank when this block ends.
Section B29 minutesIn order. Any question that stalls for more than 90 seconds gets a star and gets left. Come back only if there is time.
Final check3 minutesBlanks, then units, then anything unreasonable. In that order.

The two checkpoints

Two glances at the clock in the whole paper, not continuous monitoring, which costs attention and raises anxiety without improving pace.

  • At 18 minutes. Section A should be finished and Section B started. If Section A is not finished, choose from the remaining options immediately and move on. Section B carries more marks per question.
  • At 40 minutes. Should be into the last quarter of Section B. If not, skip forward to any remaining short questions and pick up the easy marks before returning.

What to do when stuck

The instruction, rehearsed until it is automatic, is: read it once more, underline what it is asking, and if there is still nothing after fifteen seconds, star it and move on. The reason this needs rehearsing is that the instinct is the opposite. A child who is stuck usually stares harder, and a paper is lost three minutes at a time.

The one thing that must be finished is Section A. It is the fastest section and it is the one where a blank is guaranteed to score zero while a guess is not. If the paper is going badly, the correct response is to make sure every multiple choice box has a letter in it.

Section 13

The three mocks, and how to mark them

MockWhenWherePurpose
Mock 1Session 13, w/c 14 DecIn the sessionFirst full-length experience. Establishes a baseline mark and, more usefully, shows where the time goes. I watch rather than mark.
Mock 2Tue 29 DecAt homeTests whether performance holds after two weeks of reduced practice. Also rehearses sitting a paper in an unfamiliar setting.

Add the specimen paper as a fourth, sat as soon as it arrives, whenever that is. It is the only paper of the four that is certain to match the real one in style.

Conditions, every time

  • Fifty minutes, timer visible, one sitting, no breaks.
  • No calculator, no times tables square, no help of any kind.
  • Pen or pencil, ruler, eraser. Nothing else on the desk.
  • Started and finished in one go, even if it goes badly. Stopping halfway teaches the wrong thing.

Marking

Three numbers get recorded each time, not one.

  1. The mark. Useful only for the trend across three papers. A single mock score means very little.
  2. Questions unattempted. Recorded separately from questions wrong, because they are a pacing problem and not a knowledge problem, and they have a completely different fix.
  3. Marks lost to slips. Questions where the method was right and the arithmetic was not. If this number is high, the answer is sprint work and slower written layout, not more topic teaching.

The repair list

Never more than five items, chosen by how many marks they are likely to be worth in January rather than by how badly they were done. A topic worth two marks that was catastrophically failed comes below a topic worth eight marks that was half right. One item per day for the following week, then the item goes into the error book re-test cycle.

On mock scores and morale

Mock 1 in mid-December will very likely be the lowest of the three, because it is the first timed full paper and the child will not have finished it. Say this before the paper, not after. A child who is told in advance that the first mock is expected to be unfinished treats it as information. A child told afterwards treats it as consolation.

Section 14

When the specimen paper arrives

The school says a specimen paper will be sent to all Year 7 applicants. It is the most valuable single document in this whole process, and the plan should bend to it. Chase it if it has not arrived by the end of October.

The audit, twenty minutes, done by me before the next session

  1. Count the marks by strand. Number, Algebra, Shape, Handling Data. Compare against the weighting this plan assumes and adjust the remaining sessions to match. If Shape turns out to be a fifth of the paper rather than a tenth, Session 9 grows.
  2. Count the questions and the marks in each section. This converts the provisional timing plan in Section 12 into an exact one: minutes per question in Section A, and minutes per mark in Section B.
  3. Note the question styles, not just the topics. Whether the written questions are single-step or multi-step, whether context-heavy word problems appear, whether explain questions appear and how many marks they carry, whether diagrams are given or have to be drawn.
  4. Note the level of the arithmetic. Whether long multiplication of two two-digit numbers appears, whether division produces decimal answers, how large the numbers get. This sets the sprint sheet difficulty for the rest of the term.
  5. Note the layout. Answer boxes, working space, whether multiple choice answers are circled or written in a grid. Small things, but a child meeting an unfamiliar answer format for the first time in the exam loses time to it.

Then

The child sits it under full conditions at the next available weekend, and the remaining scheme of work is rewritten against what the audit found. Any topic in this plan that does not appear anywhere in the specimen paper drops down the order. Any topic that appears twice moves up.

One caution. A specimen paper is one paper, not the syllabus. A topic listed in the school's published content but absent from the specimen still gets taught, just later. The specimen tells you about style and weighting with confidence, and about content only with caution.

If a session is missed and cannot be rescheduled, do not compress two topics into the following hour. Cut one topic from the list above instead and keep the standard hour intact. Section 16

The parent page

This page can be printed on its own and handed over. Everything on it is a request, and every request has a reason attached, because instructions without reasons do not survive a difficult Tuesday.

The six things that matter

  1. A fixed twenty-minute slot, five days a week, same time each day. Same place, table cleared, no screens. The consistency is doing more work than the content is. A slot that moves around gets negotiated, and a slot that gets negotiated gets skipped.
  2. Mark it the same day. Ticks and crosses only. Circle the crosses. Do not explain, do not correct, do not re-teach. A method explained differently from the one taught in the lesson leaves the child holding two half-methods, which is worse than one whole one. Send me a photo instead and I will deal with it in the next hour.
  3. If they are stuck and upset, stop. Write the question number down and move on. Twenty minutes is the ceiling. A distressed forty minutes costs more in December than the marks it might buy in January.
  4. Do not add extra work. No additional worksheets, no revision apps, no practice books bought in a moment of worry. The volume in this plan is set deliberately and the retrieval schedule depends on it. Extra material dilutes the spacing and adds fatigue.
  5. Send me the sprint scores each week. One line is enough. It is the fastest early warning signal in the whole plan, and a score that stops improving tells me something is wrong before a mock will.
  6. No maths on Friday 15 January. None. The evening before an exam is for sleep and for a normal evening. Nothing learned that night will be available the next morning, and anything got wrong that night will still be in their head at nine o'clock.

Two things worth saying out loud to the child, once

  • That the mock in December is expected to be unfinished, and that this is normal and planned for.
  • That this exam tests work they have already met at school, not new material, and the preparation is about speed and accuracy rather than about learning something hard.

What I will send you

  • A weekly home practice sheet, handed over at the end of each session.
  • A short note after each mock: the three numbers, and the five repair items.
  • A photograph or scan of the marked mock within 24 hours.
  • A message if the sprint score plateaus for three weeks, before it becomes a problem.

How to answer "am I going to fail?"

The honest and useful answer is that the exam is testing Year 5 and Year 6 work, that we are working through it in order between now and January, and that the job between now and then is twenty minutes a day. Specific, factual, and about actions rather than outcomes. Reassurance that sounds like reassurance tends to confirm the worry rather than settle it.

Appendix B

Methods card: the things to know cold

These are recall, not understanding. Each one should come back without hesitation, because a child working these out from first principles in a 50-minute non-calculator paper will not finish. Tested in starters throughout the term.

Number

  1. Times tables to 12 × 12, and the matching division facts.
  2. Square numbers to 144, and their square roots.
  3. Prime numbers up to 50. And that 1 is not prime.
  4. Divisibility tests for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9 and 10.
  5. Rounding: underline the digit, look at the one to its right, 5 or more rounds up.
  6. Order of operations: brackets, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.
  7. Negatives: adding moves right along the number line, subtracting moves left.
  8. Multiplying by 10, 100, 1,000 moves the digits one, two, three places left.
  9. Fraction of an amount: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.
  10. Simplifying: divide top and bottom by the highest common factor, then check again.
  11. Adding fractions: same denominator first, then add the tops only.
  12. Percentages without a calculator: 50% halve, 25% quarter, 10% divide by ten, 5% half of ten per cent, 1% divide by a hundred. Build everything else from these.
  13. Percentage decrease: find the percentage, then subtract it from the original.
  14. The conversions: a half is 0.5 and 50%, a quarter 0.25 and 25%, three quarters 0.75 and 75%, a fifth 0.2 and 20%, a tenth 0.1 and 10%, a third about 0.33 and 33.3%.
  15. Ratio: add the parts, divide the total by that number, multiply by each share.
  16. Proportion: find the value for one, then scale it.

Shape

  1. Perimeter is the distance round the outside. Area is the space inside.
  2. Rectangle area = length × width.
  3. Triangle area = half × base × perpendicular height.
  4. Parallelogram area = base × perpendicular height. Never the slanted side.
  5. Compound area: split it into rectangles, or subtract from the bounding rectangle.
  6. Angles on a straight line add to 180 degrees.
  7. Angles at a point add to 360 degrees.
  8. Angles in a triangle add to 180 degrees.
  9. An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal base angles.
  10. Metric: 10 mm in a cm, 100 cm in a m, 1,000 m in a km, 1,000 g in a kg, 1,000 ml in a litre.
  11. Area is measured in squared units. Perimeter is not.
Appendix B, continued

Algebra, data and technique

Algebra

  1. Coordinates are (x, y): along first, then up or down.
  2. Quadrant signs: upper right both positive, upper left x negative, lower left both negative, lower right y negative.
  3. Sequence rule: find the difference between consecutive terms, and check it across three gaps, not one.
  4. Substituting: replace the letter with the number, then obey the order of operations.
  5. Inverse operations: undo the operations in reverse order.

Handling data

  1. Mean = total divided by how many there are.
  2. Median = order the list first, then take the middle.
  3. With an even number of values, the median is the mean of the middle two.
  4. Mode = the most common value. There may be none, and there may be two.
  5. Range = largest minus smallest. It is a difference, not a value from the list.
  6. Pie chart: the fraction of the circle multiplied by the total. Or 360 divided by the total gives the degrees for one item.

Technique

  1. In Section A: cover the options, solve, then look.
  2. Nothing is left blank in Section A.
  3. In Section B: working above the answer, unit after it.
  4. Stuck for fifteen seconds: star it and move on.

That card, and nothing else, is what gets reviewed on Monday 11 January and Thursday 14 January. Ten is the limit. A revision list of forty-two items in the final week is a list that gets read rather than learned.

What I need before Session 1

Documents

  • The most recent school report, the maths section, including any teacher comment and any level or band given.
  • The child's current maths exercise book, or clear photographs of two recent pages, including one with corrections on it. The corrections are more informative than the ticks.
  • The specimen paper, the moment it arrives. If it has not arrived by the end of October, ask the school for it.
  • The school's own letter confirming the exam date, start time and location.
  • Confirmation of whether extra time or any other access arrangement has been applied for or granted. If the child has a diagnosis or a school plan, this needs applying for now rather than in December.
  • Dates of any other entrance exams the child is sitting, so the schedule can account for them.
  • Details of any other tutoring or extra maths already happening, and with whom. Two tutors teaching two methods is worse than one.

Questions for the parent, answered before or at Session 1

  1. What does the child say about maths when nobody is trying to encourage them?
  2. Has anything gone wrong in maths recently? A test, a change of set, a change of teacher, a comment from someone. Recent history explains a lot of what a diagnostic shows.
  3. Which topics or question types does the child avoid, put off or get upset by?
  4. How do they respond to being timed? Some children speed up and some freeze, and the plan is paced differently for each.
  5. What has the child actually been told about this exam and why it matters? I need to know what they are carrying before I add anything to it.
  6. What time of day can the twenty minutes reliably happen, five days a week, from next week until January?

What you get back

Within 48 hours of Session 1: the strand grid filled in, the sprint baseline, the revised order of Sessions 2 to 12 with any reasons for the changes, and the first week's home practice. If the diagnostic shows anything that materially changes the picture, such as a fluency score low enough to reshape the term, I will say so plainly rather than absorb it quietly into the schedule.

The honest position, as of today

Sixteen weeks, fifteen hours of tuition and roughly twenty-six hours of home practice is a substantial amount of preparation for a paper pitched at levels 4 and 5. The content is not the difficulty. What decides the outcome is arithmetic fluency without a calculator, finishing within fifty minutes, and whether the twenty minutes a day actually happens. The first of those the lessons can build. The second the mocks can train. The third is the one thing in this plan that is outside my control, and it is the one that matters most.
