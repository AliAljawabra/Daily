What changed in this revision

Every practice question is now sourced, not invented. The diagnostic, the mocks, and every week's home practice point to a real, named, freely available paper or worksheet set — official KS2 SATs papers, independent-school specimen papers with their own mark schemes, and topic-by-topic worksheets from Corbettmaths Primary. I am not qualified to certify that a question I wrote myself sits precisely at National Curriculum level 4 or 5; a published SATs paper or school specimen paper already carries that guarantee. Appendix A lists every source with a direct link.

The timing is looser. The old minute-by-minute run sheet for each hour has gone. Section 4 now describes four loose phases with rough proportions, not a stopwatch schedule, because a rigid nine-part hour is not how a ten-year-old's attention actually works.

Section 1

The exam, and what it is really testing

Read the specification closely and the content list is unremarkable. Everything named sits inside the Year 5 and Year 6 curriculum. The difficulty is not in the topics.

The exam is 50 minutes, split into a multiple choice section worth about 40 per cent of the marks and a written section worth about 60 per cent. No calculator. The content spans four strands: Number, Algebra, Shape, Handling Data.

Three features of that design decide how the preparation should work.

It is an arithmetic test wearing four different hats

Remove the calculator and almost every question, whatever strand it sits in, ends in a piece of mental or written arithmetic. Area of a triangle is one multiplication and one halving. Percentage of an amount is a division and a multiplication. Mean is an addition and a division. A child who is secure on method but slow or inaccurate with number will lose marks across all four strands at once, and the mark scheme will make those look like four separate weaknesses when they are one.

This is why arithmetic fluency, not topic coverage, is the spine of the plan.

Fifty minutes is short

Fifty minutes across two sections means the child cannot afford to stall. Most candidates at this level lose more marks to unfinished papers and careless slips than to topics they have never met. Pace is a trainable skill and it is trained by repeated timed exposure, not by being told to work faster.

Multiple choice is a different exam from written answers

Section A rewards speed, estimation, elimination and working backwards from the options. Section B rewards laid-out method, because method marks exist there and nowhere else. These need separating and practising as two distinct skills. A child drilled only on written method will be slow in Section A. A child drilled only on multiple choice will write bare answers in Section B and lose the method marks.

What the plan therefore prioritises, in order

  1. Arithmetic accuracy and speed without a calculator
  2. Fractions, decimals, percentages and ratio, which carry the heaviest mark load in Number
  3. Timed exam exposure, starting early and repeating often
  4. Area, perimeter, angles and averages, which are high yield and quick to secure
  5. Algebra and chart interpretation, which are narrow at this level and can be taught late
Section 2

How this plan is built

1. Every question comes from somewhere real

Nothing in this plan is a maths question I made up. The diagnostic, the mocks and the weekly practice are all real, published material: official Key Stage 2 SATs papers, independent-school 11+ specimen papers with their own official mark schemes, and topic worksheets from an established primary maths resource. Each of these is calibrated by people whose job that is, marked against a scheme I did not write, and pitched at exactly the level the school's own specification describes. I write the teaching, the sequencing and the technique. I do not write the questions.

2. The hour teaches. The week practises.

Fifteen hours of tuition between now and January is not enough on its own. Twenty minutes a day, five days a week, over sixteen weeks is roughly twenty-six hours of additional practice. The home practice is where fluency is built. The lesson is where method is taught, errors are diagnosed and technique is rehearsed. If the lesson is spent on drill that could have happened at home, the hour has been wasted.

3. Diagnose before teaching anything.

Session 1 is a full baseline, not an introduction. Without it the topic order is guesswork, and with one hour a week there is no room for guesswork. The diagnostic in Section 6 uses a real past paper and is designed to produce a per-strand picture, not a single mark, so the output is a priority list rather than a grade.

4. Space the retrieval.

A topic taught in week three and never revisited is gone by January. Every home practice week includes retrieval pulling from earlier topics, and every lesson opens with a quick recap of something from a previous week. Nothing is taught once.

5. Build up to exam conditions gradually, not all at once.

Full timed papers come later in the term, once the content is in place. Early sessions are untimed and low-pressure, so the child is learning maths rather than learning to feel anxious about a clock. Timing is introduced in stages: first just noticing how long something takes, then a single timed question, then a full paper, well before January.

6. Work from errors, not from topics.

After Session 1, what gets taught is decided by what the child actually gets wrong, logged in the error book and re-tested on a schedule. The scheme of work in Section 7 is the default order. The error book overrides it.

What this plan deliberately does not do

  • It does not cover Year 7 or Year 8 content. The specification is explicit that the paper sits at National Curriculum levels 4 and 5. Teaching beyond that buys nothing and costs confidence.
  • It does not increase the volume of home practice as January approaches. Twenty minutes a day is the whole term. Volume spikes in the final fortnight produce fatigue, not marks.
  • It does not use a calculator at any point, including for checking. The exam does not, so the preparation does not.
  • It does not run the child through a fixed minute-by-minute schedule every week. See Section 4.

Half term, week commencing 26 Oct. I would keep the session. A free weekday morning is the best slot of the term and percentages is a topic worth full attention. If the family is away, move Session 6 into the Christmas fortnight rather than dropping it.

Where Session 15 sits in exam week

The exam is Saturday 16 January. Hold Session 15 on Tuesday 12 or Wednesday 13 January, not Thursday or Friday. The last two days before the paper are for rest and light review, not for new work or for discovering a weakness with no time left to fix it.

Section 4

The session, not the stopwatch

The child is ten. An hour built as a rigid nine-part, minute-timed sequence is a plan for an adult sitting an exam, not a plan for a child learning one. What follows is a loose shape, not a schedule, and it moves at the pace the session actually needs.

Every session has the same four phases, roughly in this order, with no clock on the table except when a phase specifically calls for one. The proportions below are a guide for me, not an instruction read aloud to the child.

RoughlyPhaseWhat happens
First 5–10 minWarm upQuick-fire recall, no pressure, done out loud: times tables, a couple of number bonds, one or two questions from a previous week. The point is to arrive, not to perform.
Next 25–35 minThe main topicWorked example together, then the child tries similar ones with me watching the working, not just the answer. This is the bulk of the hour and it stretches or shrinks depending on how the topic is landing. If focus is dropping, we stand up, stretch, or switch to something hands-on for a minute rather than pushing through.
Last 10–15 minTrying it exam-styleA handful of questions from that week's real worksheet or paper, done the way the exam will ask for it — written out properly, or multiple choice without seeing the options first. Only from around Session 5 onward does this occasionally include a short timed burst, and even then it is two or three questions, not a full timed block. See Section 12 for how timing is introduced gradually across the term.
Last few minHandoverWhat went well, what to send home, and one thing to tell Mum or explain back to her by the end of the week.

Three rules for the hour

  • The child holds the pen. If I am writing and they are watching, they are not learning the method, they are watching me remember it.
  • A wrong answer gets talked through before it gets corrected. The wrong answer usually contains the misconception, and that is the thing worth treating.
  • If the child is tired, flat, or it has been a long school day, the hour bends. Less new content, more of what they already find satisfying, and we pick the thread back up next week. One quiet hour costs nothing over a sixteen-week term. A child who starts dreading Tuesdays costs a great deal.

Why there is no minute-by-minute schedule

A fixed sequence of seven or eight timed sub-blocks assumes an attention span and a tolerance for being timed that most ten-year-olds do not have, especially at the end of a school day. It also removes my judgement from the one variable that actually matters in the room: how the child is doing today. The four phases above give the session a shape without pretending an hour with a child runs like an exam.

Online or in person

If sessions run online, I need to see the page as it is being written, not just hear it talked through — a second device or a phone propped over the paper works well. Everything else about the hour is unchanged.

How I read the result

The arithmetic paper. This is the clearest fluency signal in the whole diagnostic, because it is exactly the kind of non-calculator arithmetic the real exam needs throughout. A strong score there means fluency is not the constraint and the scheme of work runs roughly as written. A weak score means the daily facts practice in Section 8 matters more than any single topic, and one or two teaching sessions may compress to make room for it.

Any strand in the reasoning selection. Roughly under half correct: teach that strand from the beginning, allow extra time. Roughly half to three quarters: one session to consolidate, then it comes back through retrieval only. Comfortably secure: no dedicated teaching session, just a retrieval question here and there, and the freed time goes to a weaker strand.

Section 7

Scheme of work, session by session

Sessions 2 to 12 run on the loose shape from Section 4. What changes each week is the topic. Sessions 13, 14 and 15 are built around the three mocks. The order below is the default and is rewritten at the end of Session 1 against the strand grid. Every home practice line below names a real Corbettmaths Primary worksheet — the direct links are in Appendix A, organised in the same order as this scheme.

Session 2w/c 28 Sep · Number
Written methods for all four operations, rounding, and order of operations.

Teach: column addition and subtraction including decimals of unequal length and exchanging across zeros; short multiplication; two-digit by two-digit multiplication; short division including remainders expressed as decimals; rounding to 10, 100, 1,000 and one decimal place; brackets, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.

Errors to pre-empt: decimal points not aligned; 5,002 minus 1,438 style exchanging; remainders left as r 3 when a decimal is wanted; working left to right through a mixed operation string.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Column Addition & Subtraction, Short Multiplication, Short Division, Order of Operations, Rounding (one or two of these across the week, not all five). Plus daily times tables. Explain back by Friday: why you line up the decimal points.
Session 3w/c 5 Oct · Number
Place value, decimals, and negative numbers.

Teach: multiplying and dividing by 10, 100 and 1,000 as digits moving rather than the point moving; ordering decimals of unequal length; adding and subtracting negatives on a number line; temperature and difference problems.

Errors to pre-empt: 0.7 judged smaller than 0.35 because it has fewer digits; minus 6 plus 9 treated as minus 15; the difference between minus 3 and 5 given as 2.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Multiplying/Dividing by 10, 100, 1000, Ordering Decimals, Negative Numbers. Plus a couple of Session 2 questions from the error book. Explain back: how to tell which of two decimals is larger.
Session 4w/c 12 Oct · Number
Factors, multiples, primes, square numbers.

Teach: listing factors in pairs so none are missed; multiples and lowest common multiple; highest common factor, which is the tool fractions will need next week; primes up to 50 and why 1 is not one; square numbers to 144 and their roots.

Errors to pre-empt: factors and multiples used interchangeably; 1 and the number itself omitted from a factor list; 51 and 91 assumed prime.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Factors, Multiples, Prime Numbers, Square & Cube Numbers. Explain back: the difference between a factor and a multiple, in one sentence each.
Section 7, continued

Sessions 5 to 7

Session 5w/c 19 Oct · Number
Fractions: equivalence, simplifying, of an amount, adding and subtracting.

Teach: equivalent fractions; simplifying fully using the highest common factor from last week; improper fractions and mixed numbers both ways; fraction of an amount as divide by the bottom, multiply by the top; adding and subtracting with a common denominator.

Errors to pre-empt: stopping after one simplification step; adding denominators; dividing and forgetting to multiply.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Equivalent Fractions, Simplifying Fractions, Fraction of an Amount, Adding & Subtracting Fractions. Explain back: how to find three eighths of 64 without a calculator.
Session 6w/c 26 Oct · Number · half term
Percentages, and moving between fractions, decimals and percentages.

Teach: the non-calculator toolkit — 50% by halving, 25% by quartering, 10% by dividing by ten, 5% as half of 10%, 1% by dividing by a hundred, building everything else by combining those; percentage of an amount; percentage increase and decrease; the conversions worth knowing cold: a half, a quarter, three quarters, a fifth, two fifths, a tenth, a third.

Errors to pre-empt: finding the reduction and giving it as the sale price; 15% found by halving 30%; a third written as 0.3.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Percentage of an Amount, Fractions Decimals Percentages (conversions). Explain back: three different ways to find 15% of a number without a calculator.
Session 7w/c 2 Nov · Number · timed practice
Ratio and proportion, then a first go at working against a clock.

Teach: ratio notation; total parts, one part, then scale; sharing an amount in a ratio; scaling a recipe up and down; the unitary method.

Timed practice: a short, mixed set drawn from Sessions 2–7 topics — Corbettmaths worksheets already covered, redone against a gentle time target, roughly 20 minutes. The first time timing appears at all this term. We talk about what it felt like, not just the score.

Errors to pre-empt: treating 2:3 as two thirds; giving the wrong person's share; scaling by adding rather than multiplying.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Ratio, Sharing in a Ratio. Plus whatever the timed practice flagged. Explain back: how you know which share belongs to whom.
Section 7, continued

Sessions 8 to 10

Session 8w/c 9 Nov · Shape
Perimeter and area of rectangles, triangles, parallelograms and compound shapes.

Teach: perimeter as distance round and area as space inside, established once and returned to every time; rectangle; triangle as half base times perpendicular height; parallelogram as base times perpendicular height, and why it is not the slanted side; compound shapes by splitting and by subtracting.

Errors to pre-empt: area calculated when perimeter is asked for; the halving forgotten in a triangle; the slant side used as the height of a parallelogram; units given as cm rather than cm squared.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Area of a Rectangle, Area of a Triangle, Area of a Parallelogram, Perimeter. Explain back: why the height of a parallelogram is not the sloping side.
Session 9w/c 16 Nov · Shape
Angles, symmetry, properties of shapes, metric units.

Teach: angles on a straight line summing to 180, at a point to 360, in a triangle to 180; isosceles and equilateral triangles; lines of symmetry; metric conversion between mm, cm, m and km, between g and kg, and between ml and l.

Errors to pre-empt: the two base angles of an isosceles triangle not recognised as equal; 2.5 kg converted to 250 g; a scale read as though every division is one unit.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Angles in a Triangle, Angle Facts, Symmetry, Metric Units. Explain back: the three angle facts, from memory, without prompting.
Session 10w/c 23 Nov · Data · timed practice
Mean, median, mode and range, then a second go at working against a clock.

Teach: mean as total divided by how many; median with the list ordered first; mode including no mode and two modes; range as largest minus smallest.

Timed practice: a mixed set from the Shape and Data worksheets covered so far, again against a gentle time target rather than the real exam pace.

Errors to pre-empt: median taken from the unordered list; mean and median swapped; range given as the largest value.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Mean, Median, Mode and Range (Averages). Explain back: the first thing you do before finding a median.
Section 7, continued

Sessions 11 and 12

Session 11w/c 30 Nov · Data
Bar charts, pie charts and real-life graphs.

Teach: reading a bar chart whose axis goes up in twos, fives or tens; pie charts read by fraction of the circle; conversion graphs read in both directions; reading between gridlines.

Errors to pre-empt: every gridline assumed to be one unit; a pie chart sector counted as a number of pupils rather than a proportion; a graph read in one direction only.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Bar Charts, Pie Charts, Reading Scales. Explain back: how to work out how many people one sector of a pie chart represents.
Session 12w/c 7 Dec · Algebra
Coordinates, sequences, formulae and inverse operations.

Teach: coordinates in all four quadrants; sequences by term-to-term rule, continuing them and deciding whether a given number appears; substituting into a formula with two operations; inverse operations used to work backwards from an answer.

Errors to pre-empt: x and y transposed; a negative coordinate read with the wrong sign; a two-operation formula evaluated left to right; an inverse chain undone in the wrong order.

Home practice. Corbettmaths – Coordinates, Sequences, Substitution. Explain back: how to check a coordinate is the right way round.

If the diagnostic reorders this

The likeliest reorderings, based on the Session 1 result: a weak arithmetic paper means Sessions 4 and 12 compress or move later, and the recovered time goes to daily facts practice and the four-operations work in Session 2. A strong Number strand means Sessions 2 and 3 compress into one and the spare week goes to an extra mock in November. Shape or Data noticeably weaker than Number means those sessions move earlier, ahead of ratio, because they are quick to secure and carry marks currently being given away.

Sessions 13 to 15, and the Christmas fortnight

Session 13w/c 14 Dec · Mock 1
The first full paper under exam conditions — a Dulwich College specimen paper, sat and largely marked in the session.

We set the room up like an exam — desk cleared, no interruptions — and the child sits the paper in one go. I watch for where time gets lost and where the child stalls, without stepping in. Afterwards we mark what we can together, and I take the rest away to mark fully against the paper's own scheme, back within a day or two with a short repair list — no more than five items, because a longer list gets ignored rather than acted on.

Home practice. The repair items, spread gently across the week rather than crammed into one sitting. Nothing new introduced this week.
Christmas21 Dec to 3 Jan · no sessions
A light-touch fortnight. The aim is to stop things slipping, not to make progress.
  • A few short times-tables or 5-a-day sessions across the fortnight — ten minutes here and there, not a daily obligation.
  • Mock 2, a KS2 SATs paper, sat at home on or around Tuesday 29 December under proper conditions: one sitting, no calculator, no help. Marked by a parent against the official mark scheme and photographed to me the same day.
  • Otherwise, a real break. No worksheets, no apps, no revision guides.

If one extra session can be bought in the week commencing 28 December, use it to sit and mark Mock 2 together instead. That turns the highest-value assessment of the whole plan from a home exercise into a taught one.

Session 14w/c 4 Jan · Repair and multiple choice practice
Fix what Mock 2 exposed, then spend focused time on multiple choice technique.

We group the Mock 2 errors by category rather than by question — categories are what generalise to new questions, individual questions do not. Once that is done, we work through a set of multiple choice questions using the approach in Section 10: cover the options, solve, then look. Towards the end of the session we agree the plan for Mock 3 on Saturday 9 January, including roughly what time of day it will happen.

Home practice. A couple of mixed retrieval sets. Mock 3 on Saturday 9 January, one sitting, marked and sent to me that day.
Session 15Tue 12 or Wed 13 Jan · Final session
Narrow the focus, walk through the exam-day plan, and finish on something the child gets right.

We look at Mock 3 and pick out two or three things worth a last look — not a long list. We go over the methods card in Appendix B and settle on the handful of facts still worth one more pass. Then some mixed questions, weighted toward things the child used to get wrong and now gets right, so the session ends on a good note rather than a hard one. We finish by reading through the exam-day plan in Section 17 together: what to bring, what the morning looks like, what to do if a question won't come.

Section 8

Home practice, and what goes to Mum

Most weekdays, not literally every single one, for around fifteen to twenty minutes. The consistency matters more than the exact minute count, and there is no daily stopwatch — that belongs in the exam, not in a kitchen table routine.

RoughlyWhat happens
Two or three daysA Corbettmaths worksheet on the topic taught that week — not the whole sheet if it is long, whatever fits in the time without a fight.
One dayRetrieval — a handful of questions from a couple of weeks ago, so nothing gets forgotten.
One day, from around Session 7 onwardA short exam-flavoured go: a few questions done properly, written out or multiple choice without seeing the options first. Not against a hard clock — see Section 12 for how timing builds up gradually across the term.

Why it is built this way

  • It flexes around the week. A bad day at school, a late clubs night, a birthday party — the pack for the week has enough in it to skip a day without falling behind, not so much that missing a day feels like failure.
  • Retrieval every week. Without this, September and October are gone by Christmas.
  • The exam-flavoured day builds up slowly. By January, working through a few questions the exam way should feel ordinary rather than like a test within a test.
  • Fifteen to twenty minutes is a ceiling, not a target to beat. If it is finished in ten, the day is done. Adding more is how children come to resent a subject in November, when there is still a term to go.

Marking

A parent marks it the same day from the Corbettmaths answer sheet, ticks and crosses only, no explanation offered and no re-teaching attempted. A cross gets circled and goes in the error book untouched. The reason for the no-re-teaching rule is not that parents cannot teach it — it is that a method explained differently from the one taught in the lesson leaves a child holding two half-methods, which is worse than holding one.

If the child is stuck and frustrated, the instruction is to stop, write the question number down, and move on. I would rather see the stuck question next session than sit through a tearful evening now.

The weekend

Free, except the two mock Saturdays, 9 January and any additional one agreed.

What I send Mum, and when

One message per week, sent the same day as the session, with one PDF attached. The PDF is a single-page covering sheet I write myself, followed by the real worksheet pages for that week's topic printed straight from Corbettmaths, with the answer sheet as the last page. Everything needed to print is in that one file — nothing to go hunting for.

Section 9

The error book

One exercise book, used for nothing else. It is the single most important object in this plan, because it is what turns fifteen hours of tuition into a targeted fifteen hours rather than a generic fifteen.

What goes in it

Every question the child gets wrong, in a lesson, in home practice, in timed practice or in a mock. Nothing that was got right. One entry per error, four lines:

Line 1. The question, copied or stuck in.

Line 2. What I did.

Line 3. What I should have done.

Line 4. The rule, in one sentence, in the child's own words.

Line 2 is the one people skip and the one that matters. The wrong working is where the misconception lives. Line 4 has to be the child's sentence, not mine, because a rule they cannot phrase is a rule they have not got.

Re-testing

Each entry is dated and re-tested twice: seven days later and twenty-one days later, during that week's retrieval practice. A re-test that is correct both times gets the entry ruled through. A re-test that is wrong resets the clock and, if it happens twice, the topic goes into the next lesson's teaching block regardless of what the scheme of work says.

The category count

Every four weeks, count the entries by category rather than reading them. The categories that matter for this paper are: recall speed, written method slip, misread the question, method not known, method known but misapplied, units or labelling, and ran out of time. The distribution tells you what to fix, and it changes what to fix in a way that reading individual questions does not.

Section 10

Section A technique: multiple choice

Roughly 40 per cent of the marks. It is a different skill from the written section, and it is introduced gently from the middle of the term — not drilled hard from week one, and not left until January either.

The seven rules

  1. Cover the options. Solve. Then look. Distractors are designed to match the specific errors a child is likely to make, so reading them first pulls you toward one. Working the answer out blind and then finding it in the list removes that pull entirely. This is the single most valuable habit in Section A.
  2. If your answer is not there, you have made an error, not found a printing mistake. Go back and check the last operation first.
  3. Estimate before calculating on anything with decimals or large numbers. An estimate usually eliminates two options in five seconds and catches place value errors that would otherwise be invisible.
  4. For find-the-value questions, substitute the options back in. Four substitutions are often faster and safer than one rearrangement.
  5. Two passes. First pass, answer everything that takes under 45 seconds and put a star beside anything that does not. Second pass, return to the starred ones with the time that is left. Never let one question eat four minutes on the first pass.
  6. Nothing is left blank. On the second pass, eliminate what is impossible and choose from the rest. A one in three guess is worth more than an empty box, and there is no penalty for a wrong answer on papers of this type.
  7. Check the units and check it is sensible. A perimeter answer in cm squared is wrong. A sale price higher than the original price is wrong. Ten seconds of sense-checking recovers more marks than ten seconds of extra calculation.

How this gets trained

From around Session 7, most sessions and most weeks' home practice include a handful of multiple choice questions done the “cover, solve, then look” way, drawn from that week's real worksheet or paper. A rough sense of pace — not a hard 45-second rule — gets introduced from around Session 10, and only becomes a real timer in Session 14's practice and the mocks. By January, working through multiple choice questions this way should feel familiar rather than like a new skill under pressure.

Section 11

Section B technique: written answers

Roughly 60 per cent of the marks, and the only place method marks exist. A child who writes bare answers here is throwing away marks they have already earned.

The six rules

  1. Write the calculation, not just the answer. Even where the answer is obvious. On a four-mark question, two of those marks are usually for method, and a wrong final answer with correct working still scores. A right answer with no working can score full marks, but a wrong one with no working scores zero.
  2. Read the last line of the question first. It tells you what is being asked, which tells you what to look for in the rest of it.
  3. Underline the command word and the units. Find, calculate, explain, and how many all want different things. Explain means a sentence, and a number alone will not score.
  4. Units on every answer, every time. Pounds, centimetres, centimetres squared, grams, degrees. This is free marks and it is lost constantly.
  5. Lay working out downward, not across the page. One step per line. It is easier to check, easier to mark, and it is where the method marks are found.
  6. Answer the question that was asked. If it asks for the sale price, do not stop at the discount. If it asks for Ben's share, do not give Ana's.

Explain questions

Questions asking why or how you know appear on papers of this kind and are routinely half-answered. The structure to drill is: state the answer, then give the mathematical reason, in one sentence. For B5(a)(iii) in the diagnostic, no on its own is half a mark's worth of thinking. No, because the terms go 43, 48, 53 and 51 is not among them, is the full answer. This gets practised from Session 2 through the explain-back task in every week's home practice.

The two-minute check at the end

If two minutes remain at the end of the paper, they are spent in this order, and not on re-doing questions. First, every blank filled in Section A. Second, units checked on every Section B answer. Third, any answer that is obviously unreasonable, a negative length, a percentage over a hundred, a sale price above the original, reconsidered. Re-working a question you already answered is almost always a worse use of two minutes than these three checks.

Section 12

Pacing the paper

Pacing is a skill and it is trainable, but it is only rehearsed on real timed sittings — the mocks in Section 13 and, before that, the two timed practice blocks in Sessions 7 and 10. It is never the shape of an ordinary week. Most marks lost at this level are lost to unfinished papers and rushed slips rather than to unknown topics.

The timing plan

The exact number of questions is not known until the specimen paper arrives, so this is provisional and gets confirmed then. Working from the published weighting of 40 per cent multiple choice and 60 per cent written:

BlockTimeRule
Section A, pass 114 minutesEverything that takes under 45 seconds. Star and skip the rest. Do not stop to finish a hard one.
Section A, pass 24 minutesThe starred questions. Eliminate and choose. Nothing left blank when this block ends.
Section B29 minutesIn order. Any question that stalls for more than 90 seconds gets a star and gets left. Come back only if there is time.
Final check3 minutesBlanks, then units, then anything unreasonable. In that order.

The two checkpoints

Two glances at the clock in the whole paper, not continuous monitoring, which costs attention and raises anxiety without improving pace.

  • At 18 minutes. Section A should be finished and Section B started. If Section A is not finished, choose from the remaining options immediately and move on. Section B carries more marks per question.
  • At 40 minutes. Should be into the last quarter of Section B. If not, skip forward to any remaining short questions and pick up the easy marks before returning.

What to do when stuck

The instruction, rehearsed until it is automatic, is: read it once more, underline what it is asking, and if there is still nothing after fifteen seconds, star it and move on. The reason this needs rehearsing is that the instinct is the opposite. A child who is stuck usually stares harder, and a paper is lost three minutes at a time.

The one thing that must be finished is Section A. It is the fastest section and it is the one where a blank is guaranteed to score zero while a guess is not. If the paper is going badly, the correct response is to make sure every multiple choice box has a letter in it.

The school's own specimen paper, whenever it arrives, becomes an additional sitting, not a replacement for one of the three above — see Section 14. It is the one paper of the four that is certain to match the real one in style.

The Dulwich papers are timed at one hour rather than fifty minutes. For the first mock this does not matter — slightly more time to finish makes it a gentler introduction to a full paper. For Mock 3, closer to the real thing, we can simply stop at the fifty-minute mark and mark only what was reached by then.

Conditions, every time

  • One sitting, no breaks, timer visible but not obsessed over.
  • No calculator, no times tables square, no help of any kind.
  • Pen or pencil, ruler, eraser. Nothing else on the desk.
  • Started and finished in one go, even if it goes badly. Stopping halfway teaches the wrong thing.

Marking

Marked against the paper's own official scheme. Three things get recorded each time, not just the mark.

  1. The mark. Useful mainly for the trend across three papers. A single mock score on its own means very little.
  2. Questions unattempted. Recorded separately from questions wrong, because running out of time is a pacing problem and not a knowledge problem, and it has a completely different fix — see Section 12.
  3. Marks lost to slips. Questions where the method was right and the arithmetic was not. If this number is high, the answer is more daily facts practice and slower, clearer written layout, not more topic teaching.

The repair list

Never more than five items, chosen by how many marks they are likely to be worth in January rather than by how badly they were done. A topic worth two marks that was got badly wrong comes below a topic worth eight marks that was half right. One item at a time over the following week, then the item goes into the error book re-test cycle.

On mock scores and morale

Mock 1 in mid-December will very likely be the lowest of the three, because it is the first full paper and the child will probably not finish it. Say this before the paper, not after. A child who is told in advance that the first mock is expected to be unfinished treats it as information. A child told afterwards treats it as consolation.

Section 14

When the specimen paper arrives

The school says a specimen paper will be sent to all Year 7 applicants. It is the most valuable single document in this whole process, and the plan should bend to it. Chase it if it has not arrived by the end of October.

The audit, twenty minutes, done by me before the next session

  1. Count the marks by strand. Number, Algebra, Shape, Handling Data. Compare against the weighting this plan assumes and adjust the remaining sessions to match. If Shape turns out to be a fifth of the paper rather than a tenth, Session 9 grows.
  2. Count the questions and the marks in each section. This converts the provisional timing plan in Section 12 into an exact one: minutes per question in Section A, and minutes per mark in Section B.
  3. Note the question styles, not just the topics. Whether the written questions are single-step or multi-step, whether context-heavy word problems appear, whether explain questions appear and how many marks they carry, whether diagrams are given or have to be drawn.
  4. Note the level of the arithmetic. Whether long multiplication of two two-digit numbers appears, whether division produces decimal answers, how large the numbers get. This tells me how hard the daily facts practice should be for the rest of the term.
  5. Note the layout. Answer boxes, working space, whether multiple choice answers are circled or written in a grid. Small things, but a child meeting an unfamiliar answer format for the first time in the exam loses time to it.

Then

The child sits it under full conditions at the next available weekend, and the remaining scheme of work is rewritten against what the audit found. Any topic in this plan that does not appear anywhere in the specimen paper drops down the order. Any topic that appears twice moves up.

One caution. A specimen paper is one paper, not the syllabus. A topic listed in the school's published content but absent from the specimen still gets taught, just later. The specimen tells you about style and weighting with confidence, and about content only with caution.

Cut first, in this order

  1. Position-to-term rules for sequences. Term-to-term is enough for level 5. The general rule is a stretch topic here.
  2. Compound shapes of three or more parts. Two-part compounds cover almost everything that appears.
  3. Pie chart construction and degree calculations. The school's content list says interpreting pie charts. Interpreting by simple fraction covers most of it.
  4. Best value and complex unitary method. Straightforward proportion is the requirement.
  5. Multiplying and dividing negative numbers. Adding and subtracting negatives is the level 4 and 5 requirement. Multiplying is beyond it.
  6. Order of rotational symmetry. Lines of symmetry are the common version.

The rule for a lost session

If a session is missed and cannot be rescheduled, do not compress two topics into the following hour. Cut one topic from the list above instead and keep the standard hour intact. A rushed double lesson teaches neither topic and costs the starter and the error review, which are the two blocks that make the rest work.

Section 16

The parent page

This page can be printed on its own and handed over. Everything on it is a request, and every request has a reason attached, because instructions without reasons do not survive a difficult Tuesday. The weekly logistics — exactly what gets sent, and when — are in Section 8.

The six things that matter

  1. A regular slot most weekdays, roughly fifteen to twenty minutes. Same place, table cleared, no screens, but it does not need to be the same time every single day or survive every single evening intact — see Section 8. The consistency is doing more work than the exact minute count.
  2. Mark it the same day from the answer sheet. Ticks and crosses only. Circle the crosses. Do not explain, do not correct, do not re-teach. A method explained differently from the one taught in the lesson leaves the child holding two half-methods, which is worse than one whole one. Send me a photo instead and I will deal with it next session.
  3. If they are stuck and upset, stop. Write the question number down and move on. A distressed evening costs more in December than the marks it might buy in January.
  4. Do not add extra work. No additional worksheets, no revision apps, no practice books bought in a moment of worry. The volume in this plan is set deliberately and the retrieval schedule depends on it. Extra material dilutes the spacing and adds fatigue.
  5. A quick word if something feels off. Not a formal weekly score — just a message if practice has stalled, if a topic is causing real upset, or if something at school has changed the picture. That is the fastest early warning signal in the whole plan.
  6. No maths on Friday 15 January. None. The evening before an exam is for sleep and for a normal evening. Nothing learned that night will be available the next morning, and anything got wrong that night will still be in their head at nine o'clock.

Two things worth saying out loud to the child, once

  • That the mock in December is expected to be unfinished, and that this is normal and planned for.
  • That this exam tests work they have already met at school, not new material, and the preparation is about speed and confidence rather than about learning something hard.

How to answer “am I going to fail?”

The honest and useful answer is that the exam is testing Year 5 and Year 6 work, that we are working through it in order between now and January, and that the job between now and then is a bit of practice most days. Specific, factual, and about actions rather than outcomes. Reassurance that sounds like reassurance tends to confirm the worry rather than settle it.

Appendix B

Methods card: the things to know cold

These are recall, not understanding. Each one should come back without hesitation, because a child working these out from first principles in a 50-minute non-calculator paper will not finish. Tested in starters throughout the term.

Number

  1. Times tables to 12 × 12, and the matching division facts.
  2. Square numbers to 144, and their square roots.
  3. Prime numbers up to 50. And that 1 is not prime.
  4. Divisibility tests for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 9 and 10.
  5. Rounding: underline the digit, look at the one to its right, 5 or more rounds up.
  6. Order of operations: brackets, then multiply and divide, then add and subtract.
  7. Negatives: adding moves right along the number line, subtracting moves left.
  8. Multiplying by 10, 100, 1,000 moves the digits one, two, three places left.
  9. Fraction of an amount: divide by the bottom, multiply by the top.
  10. Simplifying: divide top and bottom by the highest common factor, then check again.
  11. Adding fractions: same denominator first, then add the tops only.
  12. Percentages without a calculator: 50% halve, 25% quarter, 10% divide by ten, 5% half of ten per cent, 1% divide by a hundred. Build everything else from these.
  13. Percentage decrease: find the percentage, then subtract it from the original.
  14. The conversions: a half is 0.5 and 50%, a quarter 0.25 and 25%, three quarters 0.75 and 75%, a fifth 0.2 and 20%, a tenth 0.1 and 10%, a third about 0.33 and 33.3%.
  15. Ratio: add the parts, divide the total by that number, multiply by each share.
  16. Proportion: find the value for one, then scale it.

Shape

  1. Perimeter is the distance round the outside. Area is the space inside.
  2. Rectangle area = length × width.
  3. Triangle area = half × base × perpendicular height.
  4. Parallelogram area = base × perpendicular height. Never the slanted side.
  5. Compound area: split it into rectangles, or subtract from the bounding rectangle.
  6. Angles on a straight line add to 180 degrees.
  7. Angles at a point add to 360 degrees.
  8. Angles in a triangle add to 180 degrees.
  9. An isosceles triangle has two equal sides and two equal base angles.
  10. Metric: 10 mm in a cm, 100 cm in a m, 1,000 m in a km, 1,000 g in a kg, 1,000 ml in a litre.
  11. Area is measured in squared units. Perimeter is not.
Appendix B, continued

Algebra, data and technique

Algebra

  1. Coordinates are (x, y): along first, then up or down.
  2. Quadrant signs: upper right both positive, upper left x negative, lower left both negative, lower right y negative.
  3. Sequence rule: find the difference between consecutive terms, and check it across three gaps, not one.
  4. Substituting: replace the letter with the number, then obey the order of operations.
  5. Inverse operations: undo the operations in reverse order.

Handling data

  1. Mean = total divided by how many there are.
  2. Median = order the list first, then take the middle.
  3. With an even number of values, the median is the mean of the middle two.
  4. Mode = the most common value. There may be none, and there may be two.
  5. Range = largest minus smallest. It is a difference, not a value from the list.
  6. Pie chart: the fraction of the circle multiplied by the total. Or 360 divided by the total gives the degrees for one item.

Technique

  1. In Section A: cover the options, solve, then look.
  2. Nothing is left blank in Section A.
  3. In Section B: working above the answer, unit after it.
  4. Stuck for fifteen seconds: star it and move on.

The ten facts card. In Session 14, pick the ten items from this list that are still not automatic and write them on a single index card. That card, and nothing else, is what gets reviewed on Monday 11 January and Thursday 14 January. Ten is the limit. A revision list of forty-two items in the final week is a list that gets read rather than learned.

The honest position, as of today

The second the mocks can train. The third is the one thing in this plan that is outside my control, and it is the one that matters most.
